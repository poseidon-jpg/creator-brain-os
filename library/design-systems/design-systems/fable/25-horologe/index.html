III. The Craft

Eleven Months on the Bench

I

Wheel Cutting

Each brass wheel is hobbed, then recut by hand to a tooth-profile tolerance of two microns. A wheel that hums on the tester is rejected; a wheel must be silent.

II

Anglage

Every bridge edge is bevelled at 45 degrees with a boxwood peg and diamantine paste. Forty hours per movement, none of it visible to anyone but the owner and us.

III

Jewelling

Thirty-one synthetic rubies are pressed to a depth of five microns. The pivot must float on oil, never touch stone - that is the whole secret of a 72-hour reserve.

IV

Réglage

The balance is poised, sprung and adjusted in five positions over three weeks of observation. We certify −1/+3 seconds per day. Then we watch it for another month.

V. The Maison

Since 1887, One Address

1887

Elias Vaucher, a wheel-cutter dismissed for working too slowly, opens a two-bench atelier at 14 Rue de l'Orbe, Le Sentier. His ledger's first line: "Precision is not speed."

1904

The Observatoire de Neuchâtel awards Vaucher's calibre 11-B a first-class rating: 0.8 seconds mean daily variation. The certificate still hangs above bench three.

1931

HOROLOGE declines an offer to produce 10,000 movements a year for an American retailer. Annual production remains at forty-one.

1978

The quartz years. The maison survives by restoring marine chronometers and refuses, in writing, to fit a battery to anything.

2019

Calibre HG-87 is introduced after nine years of development - our first new base movement in half a century, and the reason this page exists.

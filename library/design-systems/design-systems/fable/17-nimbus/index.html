Current exhibition

The Architecture of Cumulus

How warm air, given nothing but an afternoon, builds cathedrals nine kilometres tall - and lets them fall by evening.

Curated by Dr. Mariel Voss, the exhibition traces a single cumulus tower from its first thermal at 09:14 to its anvil-topped collapse at 18:51. Forty-one suspended studies, a live convection room, and the museum's largest commission to date: a full-scale cumulonimbus, on loan from the troposphere.

Exhibits in Constant Rotation

Every piece is an original. No cloud has ever been shown twice.

No. 01

Cumulus

Cumulus humilis

The fair-weather original. Flat-based, cauliflower-topped, gone by dinner. The museum keeps between forty and four thousand on view, depending on the afternoon.

Altitude500 - 2,000 m
No. 02

Cirrus

Cirrus fibratus

Ice combed into handwriting. Painted at altitudes where the air forgets to be weather. Best viewed at twilight, when it holds light the ground has already lost.

Altitude6,000 - 13,000 m
No. 03

Cumulonimbus

Cumulonimbus incus

The collection's only violent work. Eleven kilometres of ambition wearing an anvil for a crown. Contains hail, lightning, and roughly a million tonnes of lifted water.

Altitude600 - 12,000 m

· tap to make it rain ·

No. 04

Stratus

Stratus nebulosus

The minimalist wing. A single grey field, edge to edge, no beginning and no signature. Critics call it featureless; the museum calls it committed.

Altitude0 - 1,200 m
No. 05

Lenticularis

Altocumulus lenticularis

A lens ground by mountain wind, hovering perfectly still while the whole sky moves around it. Frequently reported as something else. The museum makes no comment.

Altitude2,000 - 7,000 m
No. 06

Noctilucent

Polar mesospheric veil

The highest work ever hung: ice dust at the edge of space, visible only after the sun has left the room. Acquired June 2026 for the museum's new mesosphere wing.

Altitude76,000 - 85,000 m

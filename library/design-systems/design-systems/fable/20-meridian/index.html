Chapter One

Ninety-Nine Years of Gold Leaf

On the evening of 14 November 1925, four hundred guests in white tie stepped beneath a bronze sunburst canopy and into the lobby of The Meridian for the first time. The champagne was Perrier-Jouët, the orchestra was Duke Calloway's Twelve, and the marble underfoot - a radiating fan of Belgian noir and Sienna gold - had taken stonemasons eleven months to lay.

Her architect, Vivian Ashcombe-Reyes, was thirty-one years old and had never built anything taller than a bank. She spent the hotel's entire ornament budget on the ground floor, reasoning that "a guest forgives a modest room; a guest never forgives a modest arrival."

She was right. The Meridian survived the Crash, two wars, one fire in the west pastry kitchen, and seven decades of fashion. In 2019 a three-year restoration returned every fan motif, every fluted sconce, and every inch of verdigris rail to Ashcombe-Reyes's original drawings - now framed in the mezzanine gallery, floor 1½.

Behind the Bronze Door

The Gilded Fan

During Prohibition, the room behind the lobby's bronze door was listed on the hotel plans as "linen storage." The linen in question was a forty-foot bar of Cuban mahogany, a ceiling of gold-leaf fans, and the city's best-informed bartender.

The door is open now. The fans still catch the candlelight, the stools are still low enough for secrets, and head bartender Mirella Okonkwo-Hart still stirs, never shakes, unless you insist.

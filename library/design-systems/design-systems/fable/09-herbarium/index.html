§ I

A Short History of the Archive

On the fourth of March, 1847, the surgeon and amateur botanist Dr. Edmund Wray Alderton deposited his private collection of 3,120 pressed plants with the Parish Scientific Society of Aldersgate, on the sole condition that "any person of honest curiosity, of whatever station, may consult them freely." That single cabinet of Norfolk sedges and Alpine gentians became the seed of the present archive. Within a decade the Society had acquired the adjoining bookbindery on Fetter Court, hired its first Keeper of Plates, and begun the practice - continued without interruption to this day - of recording every accession in iron-gall ink, in a ledger bound in green morocco.

The great expansion came between 1862 and 1889, when the Herbarium's corresponding collectors - ships' surgeons, missionaries, governesses, and one persistent lighthouse keeper - posted specimens from four continents. The celebrated engraver Josiah Sturm was retained in 1866 to cut copper plates of the most significant accessions; his hand-tinted folios, printed on laid paper from the Marchmont mill, remain the visual signature of the collection.

“A flower pressed is a sentence spoken twice: once by the earth, and once, more quietly, by the person who knelt to save it.” - Dr. E. W. Alderton, Preface to the First Catalogue, 1851

The archive survived the fire of 1903 (the ledgers were carried out under the Keeper's coat), two wars, and a 1968 proposal to "modernise" the reading room, which the Trustees rejected in a single sentence. Today the Aldersgate Herbarium holds 214,382 specimens, 1,908 engraved plates, and the complete field notebooks of 63 collectors. It remains, as its founder intended, open to any person of honest curiosity.

§ III · the examination table

Take Up the Reading-Glass

Every visitor to the north window is issued a brass loupe, a pair of cotton gloves, and the Keeper's standing advice: look longer than feels reasonable. Under the glass, Sturm's engraving resolves into thousands of individual burin strokes - the sori of the spleenwort become stippled constellations, and the root-stock a tangle of single confident lines.

The loupe on this page behaves as the brass one does. Pass your hand across the plate and the glass will follow, rendering the folio at four times its printed size.

Each folio is raised from its press with linen tape, photographed under raking light to preserve the bite of the plate mark, and returned within the hour. Nothing leaves the building; the light comes to the paper, never the reverse. Transcription of the iron-gall ledgers proceeds at the pace of handwriting itself - roughly one collector's lifetime per volunteer per year.

No. 4 Fetter Court, Aldersgate · Admission free, as the founder required · The Wednesday evening opening is lit, still, by the 1874 gasoliers (now discreetly electrified).

The reading room seats fourteen, at the same oak tables installed in 1852. Requests are made by paper slip; folios arrive on felt-lined trolleys within the quarter hour. First-time readers are given a short induction by the Keeper, which consists chiefly of being shown how to turn a page from the corner.

rules of the room, unamended since 1852

  1. Cotton gloves shall be worn at all times when handling folios; the Herbarium provides them in three sizes and one colour.
  2. Pencil only. The last person to bring ink into the reading room was the Bishop of Ely, in 1888, and he was not readmitted.
  3. Specimens shall not be smelled, however great the temptation. The lavender presses are the single recorded exception.
  4. Conversation shall be kept to the volume of turning paper.
  5. Any reader who discovers an error in the Catalogue earns a cup of the Keeper's tea and a line in the Corrections Ledger.

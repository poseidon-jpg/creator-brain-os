A noir in five chapters. She walked out of the Blue Room at a quarter past midnight and the city closed over her like water. I was the only one who kept looking.

I

The Client

The rain had been coming down for three days, which in Los Angeles is a kind of miracle, or a kind of warning. I was on the fourth floor of the Bradbury Annex with my feet on the radiator, watching the water braid itself down the window, when she knocked. Twice. People who knock twice have already rehearsed the conversation.

Her name was Norma Vance. Twenty-nine, a schoolteacher's coat, gloves she kept smoothing like they might testify against her. Her sister Evelyn sang four nights a week at the Blue Room on South Grand - the kind of voice, Norma said, that made married men remember they had hearts and forget they had wives.

Evelyn had finished her last set on the fourteenth, stepped out the stage door, and walked off the edge of the known world. No suitcase missing. No note. Eleven dollars still in the coffee tin.

"The police say grown women disappear all the time, Mr. Callahan. I'm telling you my sister isn't a statistic. She's a person who was frightened."

I took the case for forty a day plus expenses, which was robbery, mine of her. But it was raining, the office rent was due, and there was something in the way she said frightened - like a word she'd been carrying in her mouth for a week, afraid to set it down.

II

What the Blinds Let In

Evelyn's apartment on Carondelet Street was the tidiest crime scene I never had a warrant for. The landlady let me in for two dollars and a promise I wouldn't smoke. Everything in its place: dresses hung by color, sheet music stacked square, a philodendron somebody had watered recently. People who plan to vanish don't water the plants. People who plan to come back do.

Except for the desk. The desk was wrong. Dust everywhere in that apartment had been chased out like a bad tenant, but on the desk there was a clean rectangle the size of a ledger book, and next to it, four cigarette burns in a row. Somebody had sat there a long time, working at something, being careful. Evelyn didn't smoke. Norma told me that twice.

A ticket dated a week after she disappeared. You don't buy a ticket for a trip you don't intend to take. Which meant one of two things: somebody moved her timetable up - or Evelyn Vance had a schedule of her own, and the fourteenth wasn't on it.

III

The Blue Room

The Blue Room was the kind of club where the lighting did more lying than the customers. Downstairs on South Grand, brass door, a doorman built like a legal problem. Inside, smoke hung in flat sheets and a trumpet worked through something slow enough to be a confession.

The bartender's name was Moss and his memory cost five dollars a chapter. Chapter one: Evelyn was the best thing that ever happened to the place. Chapter two: the owner, Cornell Dane, thought Evelyn was the best thing that ever happened to him, and Dane was a man who filed people under property. Chapter three, whispered, no charge, which is how you know it's true: for six months Evelyn had been staying late on Thursdays, alone in Dane's office, and Dane didn't know.

C.D. - Thursdays, after close. Not a lover's initials. A location. Cornell Dane's office, and whatever was in it worth copying into a nine-by-fourteen ledger.

She sang "Every Time We Say Goodbye" that last night, Moss said, and she sang it like she meant the title. Then she put on her coat, kissed the piano player on the cheek, and left by the stage door wearing the only red lipstick anyone ever saw her wear.

Women don't wear a new color to disappear. They wear it to be remembered. Evelyn wanted a witness - wanted the whole room to swear she walked out that door and into nothing. The lipstick wasn't vanity. It was punctuation.

IV

The Arithmetic

Here is what six months of Thursdays buys you, if you're patient and terrified and very, very neat: every crooked number Cornell Dane ever wrote down. The Blue Room washed money for half the eastside book - protection skim, fixed fights at the Olympic, a city councilman's name where a city councilman's name should never be. Evelyn hadn't stumbled onto it. She'd been made to sing for men she was then made to forget, and somewhere in month one she stopped forgetting and started copying.

Then somebody at the club got curious about why the singer stayed late, and Evelyn's schedule collapsed. The train ticket for the twenty-first became a stage door on the fourteenth. She left the lipstick story behind her like chum in the water and went under.

Dane's men tossed the apartment on the fifteenth - after Norma reported her missing, before I got there. They took the desk's clean rectangle and found it was a decoy: a household ledger, groceries and gas. The real book was somewhere else. That's why Dane's Packard had been parked across from my office for two days. I wasn't the only one hoping I'd find her. I was just the only one who'd hate myself for it.

Some cases you solve. Some cases you just learn the shape of, the way you learn a scar in the dark.
V

The Long Goodbye

I found her the way you find anything in this city - by giving up and getting breakfast. A Greyhound depot in Glendale, six in the morning, and a woman at the end of the bench with her hair cut short and dyed the color of weak coffee, reading a paper she wasn't reading. No red lipstick. Red was for exits.

I sat down two feet away and told her Norma was safe and hadn't hired anyone else. She didn't run. She said, "You're the one from the Bradbury Annex," which meant she'd been better at following me than Dane's boys were at following her. The real ledger was already gone - mailed in three envelopes to a federal man in San Francisco whose name she wouldn't say out loud in a bus station, and she was right not to.

She asked me what I'd tell her sister. I said I'd tell her the truth: that Evelyn Vance was dead. That the woman on the 6:40 to Sacramento was somebody new, and when it was over - when Dane was a headline instead of a shadow - somebody new might write Norma a letter with no return address.

The bus came. She stood, and for a second she was every goodbye I'd ever been slow to say. "You could have sold me," she said. I told her forty a day doesn't buy that. She almost smiled, and then the door folded shut, and the bus pulled out into a morning the color of old newsprint.

I billed Norma for five days and returned the rest of the retainer. Case closed, the file says. The file lies. Some cases don't close. They just get quieter, year by year, like a song from another room.

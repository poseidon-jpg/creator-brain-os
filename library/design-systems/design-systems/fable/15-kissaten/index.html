THE AMBER TEA HOUSE — KYOTO 喫茶店琥珀

Slowly
哲学
Philosophy · Tetsugaku

One bowl, prepared completely.
Nothing else.

Kohaku serves eleven seats. There is no music, no menu board, no hurry. Tea arrives when it is ready, which is rarely when you expect it and always when it should.

We follow the old kissaten habit of doing very few things with unreasonable care. Our matcha is stone-milled each morning in Uji. Our water rests overnight in Shigaraki clay. Our sweets are made in quantities small enough that some afternoons, they simply end.

The room was a weaver's workshop for ninety years before it was a tea house. We kept the worn beams, the uneven floor, the light that arrives late through the north window. Repairs are visible. That is the point.

亭主
The Tea Master · Teishu

Fifty years of the same gesture,
never once the same.

I do not make the tea better each year. I make myself quieter, and the tea notices.

Hosokawa Renji began as an apprentice at Kohaku in 1974, sweeping the entry stones before dawn. He became the third master in 1998. He still sweeps the stones. He selects each morning's water, mills the day's matcha by hand, and greets every guest by pouring — not by speaking. Guests who return often enough are eventually given their own bowl, kept on the north shelf, repaired with gold when it breaks.

作法
How We Ask You to Sit · Sahō

Three small requests.

一 / one
静

Quiet devices

Phones sleep in a lacquer box by the door. They are always still there when you leave, and rarely missed while you stay.

二 / two
待

Let it arrive

Tea is not brought when ordered but when ready. Some bowls take four minutes. The koicha takes eleven. Both are on time.

三 / three
残

Leave a little

Finish the tea, but leave the last moment unfinished — sit a breath longer than necessary. It is the only tip we accept.
